History of the Michelin Guide: How Car Tires Created the World's Top Restaurant Rating

October 5, 2026 • 6 min read

Michelin stars are the top award in the restaurant world. Yet they were invented by... tire makers. At the start of the 20th century, few could have guessed that a free guidebook for motorists would become the ultimate "bible" of haute cuisine.

The Michelin Brothers' Marketing Move

In 1900, brothers André and Édouard Michelin released a free guide so drivers would travel more often and wear out their tires faster. It included maps, gas stations, hotels, and places to grab a bite.

At first, restaurants were simply mentioned, but in the 1920s the brothers realized the power of food. The first "star" appeared—it simply meant the place cooked very well. By the 1930s, the three-star system we know today had taken shape.

What Do the Stars Mean Today?

  • 1 star: A very good restaurant in its category (worth a stop if you're nearby).
  • 2 stars: Excellent cooking, worth a detour on your route.
  • 3 stars: Exceptional cuisine, worth a special trip to another city or even country.

Secret Weapon: Anonymous Inspectors

Michelin's signature feature is its anonymous inspectors. Nobody knows when they will arrive. They pay like regular guests and judge only one thing—the food: ingredient quality, cooking mastery, flavor harmony, the chef's personality, and consistency across different visits.

“Fun fact: one of the brand's most famous symbols—the inflatable figure Bibendum—is actually made of a stack of tires. Now he is a symbol not only of roads, but of the finest dining service in the world.”

And this entire grand phenomenon in the world of fine dining began with a simple, pragmatic idea—selling more tires.
